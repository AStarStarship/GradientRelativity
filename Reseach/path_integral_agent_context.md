## Reference physics

The propagator connects an initial quantum state to a final one. In the lecture's
nonrelativistic formulation it is expressed through amplitudes for paths joining
specified spacetime endpoints.[11] The elementary phase factor is `exp(i S[path] / ℏ)`,
where `S[path] = ∫ L dt` is the action.[11]

A diffraction probability depends on the squared magnitude of a sum of complex
amplitudes, not simply a sum of independently assigned path probabilities.[11]

> The amplitude at the detector is the sum of the amplitudes for the two paths:

In a classical-limit approximation, nearby phases normally cancel away from the
classical extremum; the stationary region is what survives the oscillatory sum.[11]
For genuinely quantum propagation, contributions beyond one classical trajectory
are retained.[11] The lecture constructs the continuum expression by taking a
limit of discretized time steps and integrations over intermediate positions.[11]

## What this does and does not establish

The following are agent-authored interpretation and proposed research gates, not
new measurements reported by the lecture:

- A formal sum over paths is not a demonstrated time-ordered algorithm in which one
  photon physically tries an infinite list of routes, then selects a destination.
- Stationary action means first-order variations vanish. It need not mean a global
  minimum, the geometrically shortest curve, or shortest travel time in every setting.
- The nonrelativistic particle propagator here is a teaching baseline. A photon,
  electron-spin, or curved-spacetime calculation needs the appropriate field theory,
  causal observables, boundary conditions, and interactions.
- Drawing ten curves to suggest infinitely many possible paths is an illustration,
  not evaluating that functional integral or predicting probabilities.
- Electron-to-electron transfer rates need source and detector states, coupling,
  geometry, recoil, and a probability calculation. Counting candidate receivers alone
  does not determine the received flux.

## Relation to the author's proposal

Cale McCollough proposes that energy can statistically transfer from any electron to
any other electron through infinitely many possible paths until a least-action outcome.
That is the author's interpretation to explain faithfully; the specific transport law
has not been derived. It must not be silently replaced by a standard path integral and
then described as proven by the standard theory.

A useful next calculation is to specify one source, two receivers, their allowed
interactions, and an action or transfer kernel. Compare proposed outcome probabilities
with a matched QED reference; include interference, absorption, and causal restrictions.
This is a proposed test, not a completed result.
