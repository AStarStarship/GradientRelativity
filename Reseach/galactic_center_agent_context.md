## Retrieved reference context

The article describes a dense nuclear star cluster **surrounding**, rather than lying
inside, the Milky Way's central black hole.[12] Its Hubble description reports more
than half a million revealed stars in that cluster.[12] The central black hole,
Sagittarius A*, is described as approximately four million solar masses.[12]

> The star cluster surrounds the Milky Way’s central supermassive black hole, which is about 4 million times the mass of the Sun.

The NASA text distinguishes the star-filled central environment, surrounding matter,
and the central black hole.[12] It also describes active galactic nuclei as systems
powered by accretion of matter onto supermassive black holes.[12]

## Limits of the inference

The following are authored assessment and research requirements, not measurements
of a black-hole interior:

- This source does not count electrons behind the event horizon.
- A dense cluster around a black hole is not evidence that most galactic electrons
  are inside that black hole. No majority-electron measurement was found here.
- A black-hole mass estimate is not an electron count. The conversion requires a
  declared composition, mass budget, particle description, and uncertainty model.
- An assumption about central receiver concentration cannot by itself derive inward
  photon capture, transition probabilities, or a least-action transport law.
- Matter outside the hole must remain visible in the explanation; a theory diagram
  with many central electron markers cannot be presented as a galaxy census.

## Relation to the author's proposal

Cale McCollough proposes that most electrons in the galaxy are in its black hole,
providing a statistical destination for electron-to-electron energy transfer.
